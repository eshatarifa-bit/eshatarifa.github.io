Research

I want to understand how soft biomaterials and sensing interfaces can work together with biosignal processing, so that the materials that touch the body support reliable signals.

Overview

Neural and biomedical interfaces depend on more than algorithms. The quality of a recorded signal also depends on the material in contact with the body and on how well that interface is characterized. My work so far approaches this from three sides: biomaterials, through my hydrogel thesis; electrochemical sensing, through my current research assistantship; and biosignal processing, through two published papers on EEG and digital pen signals.

I want to bring these together in a PhD on soft, biocompatible materials for neural interfaces and brain–computer interfaces, and on how material properties affect the signals we record.

Research Threads

1. Biomaterials and hydrogels

My undergraduate thesis developed and characterized gelatin–sodium alginate hydrogels loaded with Calendula officinalis extract as a potential wound dressing. It introduced me to designing a natural-polymer material and testing how it performs. I am interested in extending this to soft, biocompatible hydrogels for electrodes and neural interfaces.

2. Electrochemical sensing

In the Electrochemical Lab at CUET, I conduct electrochemical measurements using cyclic voltammetry and potentiostat operation, and analyze the responses for hydrocortisone detection. This is teaching me how to characterize a sensing interface, a skill that carries over to studying electrode materials.

3. Biosignal processing and brain–computer interfaces

I have worked on EEG-based emotion detection using spectrograms, and on Parkinson's detection from digital pen signals using task-specific features. Both papers are published with IEEE and gave me experience in feature extraction, machine learning and signal analysis. I am a member of BrainRot Lab, a brain–computer interface research group.

PhD Direction

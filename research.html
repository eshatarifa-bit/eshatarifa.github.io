Research

I want to understand how soft biomaterials and sensing interfaces can work together with biosignal processing, so that the materials that touch the body support reliable signals.

Overview

Neural and biomedical interfaces depend on more than algorithms. The quality of a recorded signal also depends on the material in contact with the body and on how well that interface is characterized. My work asks how these pieces fit together: what changes when a signal is recorded through a soft hydrogel rather than a rigid electrode, and how electrochemical measurements can tell us whether an interface is behaving as it should.

My work so far approaches this from three sides. My published papers take on biosignal processing, in EEG and digital pen signals. My undergraduate thesis takes on biomaterials, through a hydrogel wound dressing. My current research assistantship takes on electrochemical sensing. I see these as related questions about the same underlying goal: reliable biosignals from soft, biocompatible interfaces.

Research Threads

1. Biosignal processing and brain–computer interfaces

I have worked on EEG-based emotion detection using spectrograms, and on Parkinson's detection from digital pen signals using task-specific features. Both papers are published with IEEE and gave me experience in feature extraction, machine learning and signal analysis. I am a member of BrainRot Lab, a brain–computer interface research group.

2. Biomaterials and hydrogels

My undergraduate thesis developed and characterized gelatin–sodium alginate hydrogels loaded with Calendula officinalis extract as a potential wound dressing. It introduced me to designing a natural-polymer material and testing how it performs. I am interested in extending this to soft, biocompatible hydrogels for electrodes and neural interfaces.

3. Electrochemical sensing

In the Electrochemical Lab at CUET, I conduct electrochemical measurements using cyclic voltammetry and potentiostat operation, and analyze the responses for hydrocortisone detection. This is teaching me how to characterize a sensing interface, a skill that carries over to studying electrode materials.

PhD Interests

1. EEG and brain–computer interfaces

Decoding EEG and other biosignals with signal processing and machine learning, and understanding how the recording interface affects signal quality.

2. Polymers in biomedical applications

Natural and synthetic polymers and hydrogels for wound care, drug delivery and neural interfaces, including photocurable and printable materials, and how their composition sets their mechanical, swelling and degradation behavior.

3. Electrochemical sensing for biomedical uses

Electrochemical reading and detection with cyclic voltammetry and potentiostat measurements, for example detecting biomarkers such as hydrocortisone, and characterizing electrode–material interfaces.

Where they meet: soft, biocompatible polymer-based interfaces that let us read biosignals reliably, together with the electrochemical and signal-processing methods to test them.
